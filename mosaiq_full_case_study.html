NDA-safe deep dive. A detailed walkthrough of the design explorations, iterations, and UI patterns that made large-scale research workflows easier to manage — and AI outputs easier to trust.

Users & goals

Our primary audience was finance consultants: highly skilled at interpreting markets and responsible for producing reliable, high-impact recommendations for clients and internal stakeholders.

Key needs we designed for

  • Collect, store, and browse large volumes of information without losing context

  • Synthesize information quickly — and verify what the AI used to form an answer

  • Organise findings into structured, repeatable deliverables (memos, reports, summaries)

Case study

Collecting and managing large volumes of data

Early on, the platform supported fewer file types, analysis was slower, and the assistant’s scope was mostly limited to uploaded files (not the web). As customers scaled their research, the experience started to break: uploading was inconsistent, browsing was slow, and selecting the right subset of files for analysis wasn’t obvious.

How might we…

  • Help users collect, find, share, and browse documents in as few steps as possible?

  • Make it fast to filter and reorder large databases without creating UI clutter?

Before: limited file support (PDFs, web pages, links) and basic database browsing

After: broader file support + tabs and filters for faster skimming

Design

Inspired by patterns from Finder and Gmail, the project page was redesigned into a more navigable “research hub”: clearer structure, faster selection, and better feedback when uploads fail.

  • Added checkboxes to support selecting one or multiple files quickly

  • Introduced tabs to group content and reduce scanning effort

  • Added filters positioned above tabs for fast “skim and narrow” behaviour

  • Improved failure states for restricted or unparseable content (especially paywalled web pages)

MosaiQ extension with multiple tabs selected for quick capture

Users could add content in two ways:

  • From inside the project: use Add Content and choose a source type

  • From outside the project: use the browser extension to capture multiple pages during early research

Case study

Synthesising data — AI interactions

Synthesising information is where trust gets tested. As the assistant became more capable, the UX challenge expanded from “can it answer?” to “can users understand and verify what it did?” We supported three ways to retrieve insights:

  • Automatic AI insights (summaries + key points)

  • Chat with the assistant (queries + follow-ups)

  • AI Modules (pre-configured tasks for repeatable outputs)

Automatic AI insights

Each file surfaced a fast “understand it now” layer: summary and key points. Cards could be copied, or saved for later synthesis.

Saved cards lived in Saved Insights, nested under their source file so users could reopen context and keep provenance intact.

Asking the AI

As conversations became deeper and more multi-threaded, we had to design for both psychology and mechanics: users needed to stay oriented (what’s selected, what’s being analysed) and verify outputs quickly.

How might we…

  • Make it obvious which files the assistant is using — at all times?

  • Support a more natural “conversation” flow with follow-ups and tangents?

  • Help users trace answers back to sources (and understand context)?

Selected files switched to a darker background to strengthen the selected/unselected distinction. We also introduced a clear cue — “Reply to | 4 files” — so users always knew what the assistant was analysing (especially helpful when working inside a large database and switching context often).

We iterated on “reasoning visibility” patterns. Ultimately, the Logic CTA became the right balance: available when needed, but not always visible (to avoid noise and preserve conversational flow).

We placed reasoning inside the chat area because it’s part of the interaction — not separate documentation.

Research rarely moves in a straight line. To support natural back-and-forth and focused follow-ups, we added a Reply function so users could ask questions about a specific segment of an answer — without losing the thread.

As outputs improved, the need to verify sources became non-negotiable. Citations were expanded to support both text and non-text data, and tuned for “quick context” rather than tiny excerpts.

  • Larger citation bubbles for more readable context

  • Support for charts, graphs, and tables

  • View in source to open the original location and restore full context

Case study

Turning research into deliverables

As MosaiQ matured, the workflow expanded beyond answering questions. Customers needed consistent, repeatable outputs: reports, investment memos, and stakeholder-ready summaries.

AI Modules

AI Modules are pre-configured tasks: a structured set of instructions that produce a consistent output (e.g., “X Report”). They reduced time spent rewriting prompts and improved repeatability across teams.

Case study

Templates

Templates provided a structured canvas for assembling deliverables. Users could add sections and fill them with saved insights and validated content — reducing the blank-page problem.

How might we…

  • Offer a flexible space where users can structure deliverables in a way that matches their thinking?

Templates became the main path for report building: add sections, pull in saved insights, and refine the final narrative into something publication-ready.

Exploration

AI Modules + Templates (concept)

We explored pairing Templates with Modules: each template section becomes a prompt linked to a module. When invoked, the deliverable keeps the template structure while sections are auto-populated by the AI.

This is an exploration and was not developed.

Exploration

Iterating on a flexible canvas

Another direction explored a more modular “tiles” canvas to better match how people assemble narratives: move pieces, group themes, and evolve a report structure as research changes.
